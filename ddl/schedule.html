Topics & readings

Learn the foundations, understand a central paper, examine what came next.

These 19 topics are candidates for the seminar. Only the topics students and the instructor choose will enter the final 13-week schedule. Selection, order and presenter assignments are TBD.

Each selected topic gets two academic hours and one focused question. The main paper is the center; background and recent comparisons are selective reading, not several full-paper presentations. Alternative focuses within a topic are choices for the same session.

All students complete one required shared pre-reading, chosen by the presenters in consultation with the instructor and announced one week before class. Allow 30–45 minutes for a tutorial, a short paper or specified sections. Presenters include two guiding questions; each student brings one question or point of confusion. The main paper is optional for the audience unless selected as the shared pre-reading. Specific pre-readings are TBD.

TTT centers on learning as neural memory, with hypernetworks as an optional comparison; distribution-shift adaptation with the original Sun et al. paper is an alternative focus, and IT³ is supplementary. Mechanistic interpretability combines feature foundations with one circuit-tracing case; representation alignment is an optional comparison. Drifting and IMLE can share a session on one-step generation, with one main mechanism developed in depth. Neither topic reserves two weeks. Flow matching is assumed background from the base course.

Topic family 01

Kernels, feature learning & optimization

Candidate topicTwo academic hours

NTK and feature learning

When can a neural network be understood as a kernel method, and when do its features change?

Background & foundations

Lee et al., Wide Neural Networks of Any Depth Evolve as Linear Models Under Gradient Descent (2019), selected sections on Taylor linearization and squared-loss dynamics. Review Jacobians, Gram matrices and gradient flow.

Main paper

Jacot, Gabriel & Hongler, Neural Tangent Kernel: Convergence and Generalization in Neural Networks (2018).

Recent development & discussion

Haas et al., On the Surprising Effectiveness of Large Learning Rates under Standard Width Scaling (2025).

Presentation focus

Derive function-space gradient flow and its solution for squared loss, then compare a finite network with its linearization. Use the recent paper to examine why width, parameterization, learning rate and the choice of loss matter for claims about feature learning.

Candidate topicTwo academic hours

μP, scaling and hyperparameter transfer

How should initialization and updates scale as networks become wider or deeper?

Background & foundations

Yang & Hu, Tensor Programs IV: Feature Learning in Infinite-Width Neural Networks (2021), selected sections on parameterization and feature learning. Review the NTK linearized regime as contrasting background; a separate NTK seminar is optional.

Main paper

Yang et al., Tensor Programs V: Tuning Large Neural Networks via Zero-Shot Hyperparameter Transfer (NeurIPS 2021; arXiv 2022).

Recent development & discussion

Dey et al., Don't Be Lazy: CompleteP Enables Compute-Efficient Deep Transformers (2025). Optional theory: Chen, Yang, Zhao & Gu, Global Convergence and Rich Feature Learning in L-Layer Infinite-Width Neural Networks under μ Parametrization (ICML 2025). Read the assumptions and result statement; this infinite-width analysis does not establish unrestricted finite-network SGD convergence.

Presentation focus

Work through activation and update scaling in a small multilayer network, and inspect a learning-rate transfer experiment. Discuss the additional constraints introduced by depth; width transfer alone does not establish depth transfer or effective nonlinear learning.

Candidate topicTwo academic hours

Why training works: convergence and loss landscapes

Which assumptions let us prove that training reaches a global minimum, and how close are those proofs to practical networks?

Scope for one session

One seminar of two academic hours. Choose a convergence proof for a restricted model (shown below), or the edge-of-stability dynamics focus. A survey of all optimization and generalization theory would be too broad for one meeting.

Background & foundations

Karimi, Nutini & Schmidt, Linear Convergence of Gradient and Proximal-Gradient Methods Under the Polyak-Lojasiewicz Condition (2016), the PL inequality and gradient-flow loss decay. Historical context: Choromanska et al., The Loss Surfaces of Multilayer Networks (2015; 2014 preprint), the spin-glass analogy and its assumptions. Optional contrast: Soudry & Hoffer, Exponentially vanishing sub-optimal local minima in multilayer neural networks (ICLR 2018), volumes of regions containing minima versus a training trajectory.

Main paper

Dana, Pillaud-Vivien & Bach, Convergence of Shallow ReLU Networks on Weakly Interacting Data (NeurIPS 2025). Gradient flow in finite shallow ReLU networks, weakly interacting inputs and a particular asymmetric randomized initialization.

Recent development & discussion

Petit, Poon & Peyré, On the global convergence of gradient flow for wide shallow models beyond homogeneous nonlinearities (2026). The result is conditional: if the studied Wasserstein gradient flow converges, its limit is globally optimal. Watch list: Soudry’s publication page lists Global Convergence in Deep Networks via the Second Law of Thermodynamics (NeurIPS 2026); a linked manuscript was unavailable when this list was checked, so reading and claims remain TBD.

Presentation focus

Derive loss decay from a PL inequality, then explain how the main paper maintains a trajectory-wise condition. Inspect its data, initialization, width and gradient-flow assumptions. Separate landscape geometry, reaching a minimum, selecting a solution and generalization. These results make concrete progress; they do not supply a general guarantee for finite deep networks trained with SGD or Adam.

Alternative focus: edge of stability and Central Flows

Background & foundations

Quadratic losses, Hessian eigenvalues and the gradient-descent stability threshold 2 / learning rate. Cohen et al., Gradient Descent on Neural Networks Typically Occurs at the Edge of Stability (ICLR 2021), short-term loss oscillations and longer-term progress.

Main paper

Cohen, Damian, Talwalkar, Kolter & Lee, Understanding Optimization in Deep Learning with Central Flows (ICLR 2025). Start with the gradient-descent case and the authors’ illustrated explanation.

Recent development & discussion

Berthier, The Multiple Timescales of Gradient Descent on the Edge of Stability: A Perturbative Derivation of the Central Flow (2026), selected introduction and toy example. This formal asymptotic derivation does not provide remainder bounds or a convergence proof.

Presentation focus

Derive the quadratic stability threshold, examine self-stabilization, and show how averaging oscillations produces a smoother trajectory description. Use one example to compare the discrete iterates and central flow. Explain what this reveals about optimization dynamics without treating it as a general global-convergence theorem.

Candidate topicTwo academic hours

Test-time training (TTT)

What should a model learn at inference, and which objective makes those updates useful?

Scope for one session

One seminar of two academic hours. Develop the TTT recurrent update as the central mechanism; hypernetworks offer a short comparison between learning and generating context-dependent weights. Distribution-shift adaptation is an alternate focus. Choose the comparisons that fit the question; IT³ is supplementary reading.

Background & foundations

Sun, Wang, Liu, Miller, Efros & Hardt, Test-Time Training with Self-Supervision for Generalization under Distribution Shifts (ICML 2020), selected introduction and auxiliary-task formulation. Ba et al., Using Fast Weights to Attend to the Recent Past (2016), fast versus slow weights and associative memory.

Main paper

Sun et al., Learning to (Learn at Test Time): RNNs with Expressive Hidden States (2024). A model is the recurrent hidden state, and self-supervised gradient updates are the state transition.

Recent development & discussion

Tandon et al., End-to-End Test-Time Training for Long Context (2025). Next-token updates compress context into weights, with a meta-learned initialization. Optional adaptation comparison: Durasov, Shocher, Oner, Chechik, Efros & Fua, IT³: Idempotent Test-Time Training (ICML 2025); implementation.

Presentation focus

Work through a TTT-Linear update and its recurrent computation. Use one context-memory experiment and one short comparison: end-to-end next-token adaptation or Doc-to-LoRA generating an update directly. Explain the training and inference costs. Distribution-shift adaptation is a different focus within the same topic; IT³ is an optional consistency-based example that requires its own training setup.

Alternative focus: distribution-shift adaptation

Background & foundations

Shared encoders, auxiliary self-supervision and gradient alignment. Read selected setup and theory sections of the original Sun et al. TTT paper; the stated gradient-alignment result uses smoothness and convexity assumptions.

Main paper

Sun et al., Test-Time Training with Self-Supervision for Generalization under Distribution Shifts (ICML 2020).

Recent development & discussion

Comparison: Wang et al., Tent: Fully Test-time Adaptation by Entropy Minimization (ICLR 2021). Recent optional extension: Durasov et al., IT³: Idempotent Test-Time Training (ICML 2025), prediction feedback and consistency between successive predictions.

Presentation focus

Derive the original TTT gradient-alignment intuition and examine one adaptation experiment or failure case. Compare auxiliary self-supervision with entropy minimization; use IT³ as a short additional example if time permits. Discuss uninformative consistency, model collapse and stopping adaptation.

Optional comparison: hypernetworks: learning versus generating weights

Optional readings

Background: Ha, Dai & Le, HyperNetworks (2016/17), a network generating another network’s weights; Hu et al., LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models (2021/22), selected low-rank update formulation. Recent comparison: Charakorn et al., Doc-to-LoRA: Learning to Instantly Internalize Contexts (2026). Optional task-description variant: Charakorn et al., Text-to-LoRA: Instant Transformer Adaption (2025).

Discussion focus

Reserve roughly 15 minutes within the neural-memory seminar to contrast iterative TTT updates with amortized generation of low-rank weights. Compare the conditioning information, training requirements and inference cost using one Doc-to-LoRA example. A full hypernetwork or LoRA survey is optional preparation; this comparison does not add a second main paper or meeting.

RL for reasoning with verifiable rewards

Does reward-based training discover successful reasoning behavior or change which existing behavior is sampled?

Background & foundations

Spinning Up: Part 3—Introduction to Policy Optimization, selected sections on the log-derivative policy gradient and baselines. Reserve 25–30 minutes to introduce policies, sampled completions, rewards and group-relative advantages; prior RL coursework is not assumed.

Main paper

DeepSeek-AI et al., DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning (2025), focusing on R1-Zero and the learning objective.

Recent development & discussion

Yue et al., Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model? (2025). Counterpoint: Liu et al., ProRL: Prolonged Reinforcement Learning Expands Reasoning Boundaries in Large Language Models (2025).

Presentation focus

Derive the policy-gradient identity before introducing group-relative advantages, clipping and KL regularization. Compare pass@k evidence under the papers' different training and evaluation settings; finite sampling cannot establish that a base model assigns zero probability to a solution.

Candidate topicTwo academic hours

Online continual learning: stability, plasticity and replay

How can a network keep adapting to a changing data stream while retaining useful knowledge?

Scope for one session

Two academic hours: choose OCAR or the self-supervised alternative below, develop one central derivation, inspect one streaming experiment and make one short comparison. A one-pass incoming stream can allow replay and bounded repeated updates on the current minibatch; it does not imply exactly one gradient step per observation.

Background & foundations

Chaudhry et al., On Tiny Episodic Memories in Continual Learning (2019): experience replay with limited memory. Kirkpatrick et al., Overcoming Catastrophic Forgetting in Neural Networks (PNAS 2017): elastic weight consolidation and Fisher-weighted protection. Optional mathematical background: Martens & Grosse, Optimizing Neural Networks with Kronecker-factored Approximate Curvature (ICML 2015), selected sections introducing the Fisher and one K-FAC block.

Main paper

Urettini & Carta, Online Curvature-Aware Replay: Leveraging 2nd Order Information for Online Continual Learning (ICML 2025). Focus on Sections 3–5: formulate the streaming update, constrain changes to predictions on replay data, and derive the curvature-aware step.

Recent development & discussion

Cignoni et al., CLA: Latent Alignment for Online Continual Self-Supervised Learning (CoLLAs 2026): compare representation alignment with labeled prediction constraints. Conceptual companion: Dohare et al., Loss of Plasticity in Deep Continual Learning (Nature 2024), which distinguishes forgetting old knowledge from losing the ability to learn new tasks; its broader continual-learning experiments are not all single-pass online protocols.

Presentation focus

Derive the local KL/Fisher constraint and the preconditioned update on a small example. Explain damping, memory representativeness and the difference between forgetting and loss of plasticity. Evaluate accuracy during the stream as well as at its end. Check what information each experiment provides: OCAR hides task boundaries, but its class-incremental replay-Fisher weighting uses the number of new classes per experience.

Alternative focus: Self-supervised continual representations

Background & foundations

Chaudhry et al., On Tiny Episodic Memories in Continual Learning (2019), for replay; Chen & He, Exploring Simple Siamese Representation Learning (CVPR 2021), for the SimSiam objective and stop-gradient.

Main paper

Cignoni et al., CLA: Latent Alignment for Online Continual Self-Supervised Learning (CoLLAs 2026), Sections 3–5. An unlabeled one-pass stream, hidden task boundaries, bounded replay and a fixed computational budget.

Recent development & discussion

Compare with OCAR (ICML 2025): protecting predictions versus aligning representations.

Presentation focus

Derive the self-supervised learning and alignment objective. Compare EMA-teacher and stored-feature targets, explain why alignment applies to replay samples, and inspect the consequences of a fixed computational budget. The reported budgets count processed examples rather than establishing exact equality of FLOPs.

Topic family 04

Editing & interpreting networks

Candidate topicTwo academic hours

Model merging and task arithmetic

Why can adding parameter updates combine capabilities, and when does task interference prevent it?

Scope for one session

Choose task arithmetic or Neural Thickets for one seminar of two academic hours. Both examine capabilities near pretrained weights; averaging weights and ensembling predictions have different mechanisms and inference costs.

Background & foundations

Ilharco et al., Editing Models with Task Arithmetic (2023). Review parameter differences relative to a shared pretrained checkpoint and function-space linearization from NTK background.

Main paper

Ortiz-Jimenez, Favero & Frossard, Task Arithmetic in the Tangent Space: Improved Editing of Pre-Trained Models (2023).

Recent development & discussion

Hitit, Girrbach & Akata, A Systematic Study of In-the-Wild Model Merging for Large Language Models (2025 preprint; TMLR 2026). Alternative direction: Gan & Isola, Neural Thickets: Diverse Task Experts Are Dense Around Pretrained Weights (ICML 2026), sampling, selecting and ensembling nearby task experts.

Presentation focus

Derive how adding task vectors affects a linearized model and examine the proposed weight-disentanglement mechanism. Compare controlled task combinations with heterogeneous checkpoints, including conflicting objectives and gains relative to the best individual model.

Alternative focus: Neural Thickets and gradient-free adaptation

Background & foundations

Wortsman et al., Model soups: averaging weights of multiple fine-tuned models improves accuracy without increasing inference time (ICML 2022), shared pretrained initialization, weight averaging and prediction ensembles. Recap task vectors from Ilharco et al., Editing Models with Task Arithmetic (2023).

Main paper

Gan & Isola, Neural Thickets: Diverse Task Experts Are Dense Around Pretrained Weights (ICML 2026). RandOpt samples Gaussian weight perturbations, evaluates candidates on task data, selects experts and ensembles their predictions. See the authors’ illustrated project.

Recent development & discussion

Cendra et al., Can Bayesian Optimization Efficiently Find a Strong Single Expert in Neural Thickets? (2026). A follow-up on finding one expert with fewer evaluations in the tested low-dimensional search settings.

Presentation focus

Explain expert density and diversity around a pretrained checkpoint. Work through the probability of finding a useful perturbation after repeated sampling, then compare weight averaging, expert selection and prediction ensembling. Account for task-data evaluations and ensemble inference cost. This is evidence about pretrained neighborhoods, not a proof that random initialization or unrestricted gradient descent succeeds.

Candidate topicTwo academic hours

Mechanistic interpretability: from features to circuits

How do interpretable features support a circuit explanation, and how can we test that explanation causally?

Scope for one session

One introductory seminar of two academic hours. Use superposition and sparse features as background, then develop one circuit-tracing case and intervention. Representation alignment is optional discussion material. The session centers on circuit tracing; full sparse-autoencoder and alignment analyses are optional.

Background & foundations

Elhage et al., Toy Models of Superposition (2022), one small example of sparse features sharing dimensions. Selected background: Elhage et al., A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits (2021), the residual stream; Templeton et al., Scaling Monosemanticity: Extracting Interpretable Features from Claude 3 Sonnet (2024), the sparse-autoencoder objective and one feature illustration.

Main paper

Ameisen et al., Circuit Tracing: Revealing Computational Graphs in Language Models (2025).

Recent development & discussion

Kamath et al., Tracing Attention Computation Through Feature Interactions (2025).

Presentation focus

Use a toy example to explain superposition and introduce sparse feature extraction briefly. Center the presentation on how a replacement model produces an attribution graph, one circuit case study and a causal intervention. Distinguish sparse autoencoders from the tracing method’s replacement model. Assess omitted computations and approximation error; interpreting a feature and validating a circuit are different steps.

Optional comparison: the Platonic representation hypothesis and unpaired alignment

Optional readings

Background: Kornblith et al., Similarity of Neural Network Representations Revisited (ICML 2019), centered kernel alignment. Foundational hypothesis: Huh et al., Position: The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (ICML 2024). Recent development: Schnaus, Dagès, Cremers, Wang & Isola, Shared Geometry As A Rosetta Stone: Cross-Modal Alignment Without Paired Data (October 2026 preprint); the authors’ interactive project illustrates how shared geometry permits coarse cross-modal alignment without pairs at the alignment stage. Recent critique: Gröger, Wen & Brbic, Revisiting the Platonic Representation Hypothesis: An Aristotelian View (ICML 2026). Related caution: Leask et al., Sparse Autoencoders Do Not Find Canonical Units of Analysis (2025).

Discussion focus

Use Rosetta as a practical test of a consequence of the Platonic hypothesis: is shared geometry sufficient to connect independently trained representations without paired examples? Its coarse geometric initialization and alternating assignment / Procrustes updates recover useful broad semantic structure, while finer details remain difficult. Paired samples are still used for evaluation and the CKA analysis of when alignment succeeds. Distinguish useful geometric correspondence from universal convergence, canonical features and causal circuit explanations. This is an optional discussion within the selected interpretability seminar; the full alignment derivation is further reading.

Topic family 05

Generative models

Candidate topicTwo academic hours

One-step generative models: Drifting and IMLE

How can matching real and generated samples train a generator that produces a sample in one pass?

Scope for one session

One comparative seminar of two academic hours. Use Drifting for the central derivation and IMLE / ROMS-IMLE for comparison, or choose the IMLE-first route below. Separate deeper seminars are available if both focuses are selected.

Background & foundations

Yujia Li, Swersky & Zemel, Generative Moment Matching Networks (ICML 2015), selected kernel/MMD material. Ke Li & Jitendra Malik, Implicit Maximum Likelihood Estimation (2018), the data-to-generated nearest-neighbor assignment and regression algorithm.

Main paper

Deng, He Li, Tianhong Li, Du & He, Generative Modeling via Drifting (2026). Estimate an attraction-repulsion field from real and generated samples and fit frozen drifted targets.

Recent development & discussion

Chirag Vashist & Ke Li, ROMS-IMLE: A Minimalist Approach to Competitive Single-Step Generative Modelling (2026). Compare nearest-neighbor assignment, robust losses and intermediate-resolution supervision with the drifting update. Optional theory: Esteban-Casadevall et al., Kernel-Gradient Drifting Models (2026), including Max Welling.

Presentation focus

Introduce sample-based distribution matching, derive the drifting field and frozen-target regression, and study one toy experiment. Compare IMLE’s data-to-generated matching and ROMS-IMLE’s practical changes briefly. Choose one main mechanism to derive; keep zero-drift and maximum-likelihood claims tied to their assumptions.

Alternative focus: IMLE and mode coverage

Background & foundations

Ke Li & Jitendra Malik, Implicit Maximum Likelihood Estimation (2018). Implicit distributions, nearest-neighbor assignment, mode coverage and the likelihood connection under stated assumptions.

Main paper

Chirag Vashist & Ke Li, ROMS-IMLE: A Minimalist Approach to Competitive Single-Step Generative Modelling (2026). Revisit IMLE with intermediate-resolution supervision and robust losses.

Recent development & discussion

ROMS-IMLE is the recent anchor. The authors’ method page provides additional explanation and experiments. Comparison: Deng et al., Generative Modeling via Drifting (2026), selected algorithm and toy example.

Presentation focus

Explain the direction of nearest-neighbor matching, latent assignment and alternating regression, then examine sampling cost and the ROMS-IMLE ablations. Keep the maximum-likelihood connection tied to its assumptions. Use Drifting as a brief comparison within the same two-academic-hour seminar.

Symmetry, equivariance and canonicalization

Can we enforce symmetry by putting each input into a canonical orientation?

Scope for one session

Advanced mathematical candidate. A concrete rotation or permutation example is enough to motivate the main result within two academic hours.

Background & foundations

Cohen & Welling, Group Equivariant Convolutional Networks (ICML 2016). Group actions, equivariance and weight sharing.

Main paper

Dym, Lawrence & Siegel, Equivariant Frames and the Impossibility of Continuous Canonicalization (ICML 2024).

Recent development & discussion

Shumaylov et al., Lie Algebra Canonicalization: Equivariant Neural Operators under arbitrary Lie Groups (ICLR 2025; 2024 preprint). Selected introduction and one application.

Presentation focus

Start with a group-action example, show why a canonical choice can become discontinuous under the stated assumptions, and explain how weighted frames address continuity. Leave general topology proofs and non-compact Lie-group machinery for optional reading.

Candidate topicTwo academic hours

KANs: learnable functions and scientific discovery

When does learning the functions on network edges help more than learning ordinary weights?

Scope for one session

Two academic hours: the original architecture, one scientific example and one controlled comparison. Approximation-rate proofs are optional.

Background & foundations

Kolmogorov, On the Representation of Continuous Functions of Several Variables as Superpositions of Continuous Functions of One Variable and Addition (1957), theorem statement only. Review one-dimensional spline bases using the main paper’s architecture section. Separate representation existence from efficient approximation and learnability.

Main paper

Liu et al., KAN: Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (ICLR 2025; 2024 preprint), selected sections on learned edge functions, grid refinement and one function-fitting experiment.

Recent development & discussion

Liu et al., KAN 2.0: Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks Meet Science (2024), one scientific example. Essential comparison: Yu, Yu & Wang, KAN or MLP: A Fairer Comparison (2024). Recent optional theory: Kratsios, Kim & Furuya, Approximation Rates in Besov Norms and Sample-Complexity of Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks with Residual Connections (2025), assumptions and result statements.

Presentation focus

Build a small spline-based KAN and explain its compositional bias and possible formula recovery. Compare against an MLP with matched parameters or FLOPs and a spline-equipped MLP. Ask which benefit comes from the function basis and which comes from the architecture; the representation theorem alone does not establish a practical advantage.

GNNs: message passing and graph transformers

What graph structure can a neural network distinguish, and what does global attention add?

Scope for one session

Two academic hours on message-passing expressivity and structural encodings in graph transformers. Over-squashing is a separate alternative focus, rather than another required component.

Background & foundations

Sanchez-Lengeling et al., A Gentle Introduction to Graph Neural Networks (Distill 2021), a visual primer. Xu, Hu, Leskovec & Jegelka, How Powerful are Graph Neural Networks? (ICLR 2019), multiset aggregation, GIN and the 1-Weisfeiler-Leman expressivity ceiling.

Main paper

Stoll, Müller & Morris, Generalizable Insights for Graph Transformers in Theory and Practice (NeurIPS 2025), selected theory sections and one structural-encoding ablation.

Recent development & discussion

Ma et al., Plain Transformers Can be Powerful Graph Learners (TMLR 2026; 2025 preprint). Examine the graph-specific adaptations behind the title. Optional benchmark discussion: Bechler-Speicher et al., Position: Graph Learning Will Lose Relevance Due To Poor Benchmarks (ICML 2025).

Presentation focus

Work through a pair of graphs that 1-WL-style message passing cannot distinguish. Explain how structural or positional information changes the comparison with a graph transformer. Distinguish theoretical expressivity, measured accuracy and benchmark quality; leave the full higher-order hierarchy for optional reading.

Alternative focus: over-squashing and graph geometry

Background & foundations

Kipf & Welling, Semi-Supervised Classification with Graph Convolutional Networks (ICLR 2017), neighborhood propagation. Alon & Yahav, On the Bottleneck of Graph Neural Networks and its Practical Implications (ICLR 2021), information bottlenecks and over-squashing.

Main paper

Wilson, Bechler-Speicher & Veličković, Cayley Graph Propagation (2025), an expander-based propagation template.

Recent development & discussion

Liang et al., Mitigating Over-Squashing in Graph Neural Networks by Spectrum-Preserving Sparsification (ICML 2025), a comparison of graph modification and propagation costs.

Presentation focus

Use a bottleneck graph to distinguish over-squashing from over-smoothing. Explain expander connectivity intuitively, then inspect one propagation experiment and the cost of modifying graph connectivity. A complete group-theory or spectral proof is optional.
