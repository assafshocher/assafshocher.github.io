Each selected topic gets two academic hours and one focused question, with one reading bundle. The main paper is the center; background and recent developments supply the context and discussion. Topic numbers identify candidates and are independent of the weekly schedule.

All students complete one required shared pre-reading, chosen by the presenters in consultation with the instructor and announced one week before class. Allow 30–45 minutes for a tutorial, a short paper or specified sections. Presenters include two guiding questions; each student brings one question or point of confusion. The main paper is optional for the audience unless selected as the shared pre-reading. Specific pre-readings are TBD.

The reading bundles connect foundational ideas to recent work. Use the scope and presentation focus to decide which sections to develop carefully in class. Supplementary papers support that question; every linked paper need not become a full presentation. Flow matching is assumed background from the base course.

Topics and readings

Topic 1Two academic hours

Wide networks: NTK, feature learning and μP

Kernels, feature learning & optimization

How can increasing width lead either to kernel-like training or to feature learning, and what does this imply for scaling?

Scope for one session

Two academic hours: one short NTK derivation, one worked width-scaling example, and one hyperparameter-transfer experiment. The unifying question is how parameterization changes learning, rather than two complete surveys of NTK and μP.

Background & foundations

Jacot, Gabriel & Hongler, Neural Tangent Kernel: Convergence and Generalization in Neural Networks (2018), the Jacobian kernel and squared-loss gradient flow. Lee et al., Wide Neural Networks of Any Depth Evolve as Linear Models Under Gradient Descent (2019), selected Taylor-linearization sections. Yang & Hu, Tensor Programs IV: Feature Learning in Infinite-Width Neural Networks (2021), selected parameterization and feature-learning examples; full tensor-program proofs are further reading.

Main paper

Yang et al., Tensor Programs V: Tuning Large Neural Networks via Zero-Shot Hyperparameter Transfer (NeurIPS 2021; arXiv 2022).

Recent development & discussion

Dey et al., Don't Be Lazy: CompleteP Enables Compute-Efficient Deep Transformers (NeurIPS 2025; revised 2026), selected width/depth-transfer and non-lazy-learning results. Supporting discussion: Haas et al., On the Surprising Effectiveness of Large Learning Rates under Standard Width Scaling (2025). Further theory reading: Chen et al., Global Convergence and Rich Feature Learning in L-Layer Infinite-Width Neural Networks under μ Parametrization (ICML 2025), result statements and assumptions only; this does not establish unrestricted finite-network convergence.

Presentation focus

Derive the Jacobian-kernel gradient-flow equation for squared loss, then use one small two-layer example to compare activation and update scaling in kernel-like and feature-learning regimes. Center the main paper on one learning-rate transfer experiment from a small network to a larger one. Use one CompleteP result to discuss why width transfer and depth transfer are different questions. Leave the full infinite-width and tensor-program proofs for further reading.

Topic 2Two academic hours

Why training works: convergence, landscapes and stability

Kernels, feature learning & optimization

Which assumptions let us prove that training reaches a global minimum, and how close are those proofs to practical networks?

Scope for one session

Two academic hours: derive PL-based loss decay and explain one restricted neural-network convergence argument. Reserve a short quadratic example and one plot for discrete-time stability. Full central-flow derivations and adaptive-optimizer analysis are further reading.

Background & foundations

Karimi, Nutini & Schmidt, Linear Convergence of Gradient and Proximal-Gradient Methods Under the Polyak-Lojasiewicz Condition (2016), the PL inequality and gradient-flow loss decay. Historical context: Choromanska et al., The Loss Surfaces of Multilayer Networks (2015; 2014 preprint), the spin-glass analogy and its assumptions. Optional contrast: Soudry & Hoffer, Exponentially vanishing sub-optimal local minima in multilayer neural networks (ICLR 2018), volumes of regions containing minima versus a training trajectory. Brief dynamics background: Cohen et al., Gradient Descent on Neural Networks Typically Occurs at the Edge of Stability (ICLR 2021), one oscillatory training plot and the quadratic stability threshold 2 / learning rate.

Main paper

Dana, Pillaud-Vivien & Bach, Convergence of Shallow ReLU Networks on Weakly Interacting Data (NeurIPS 2025). Gradient flow in finite shallow ReLU networks, weakly interacting inputs and a particular asymmetric randomized initialization.

Recent development & discussion

Petit, Poon & Peyré, On the global convergence of gradient flow for wide shallow models beyond homogeneous nonlinearities (2026). The result is conditional: if the studied Wasserstein gradient flow converges, its limit is globally optimal. Watch list: Soudry’s publication page lists Global Convergence in Deep Networks via the Second Law of Thermodynamics (NeurIPS 2026); a linked manuscript was unavailable when this list was checked, so reading and claims remain TBD. Supporting dynamics comparison: Cohen et al., Understanding Optimization in Deep Learning with Central Flows (ICLR 2025), the gradient-descent illustration and authors' explanation. Further reading: Berthier, The Multiple Timescales of Gradient Descent on the Edge of Stability: A Perturbative Derivation of the Central Flow (2026); its formal derivation does not supply a global-convergence proof.

Presentation focus

Derive loss decay from a PL inequality, then explain how the main paper maintains a useful trajectory-wise condition. Inspect its data, initialization, width and gradient-flow assumptions. Contrast this proof with a quadratic discrete-time stability calculation and one edge-of-stability observation. Distinguish landscape geometry, convergence, oscillatory dynamics, solution selection and generalization. Central flows describe averaged dynamics; they are not a general global-convergence theorem. Keep the recent conditional result separate from unrestricted guarantees for finite deep networks trained with SGD or Adam.

Topic 3Two academic hours

State-space models and Mamba

Sequence models & reasoning

How do recurrence, selectivity and structured matrices produce an efficient sequence model?

Background & foundations

Gu, Goel & Ré, Efficiently Modeling Long Sequences with Structured State Spaces (S4, 2021/22), selected sections on discretization and recurrence. Gu & Dao, Mamba: Linear-Time Sequence Modeling with Selective State Spaces (2023), selected sections on input-dependent dynamics.

Main paper

Dao & Gu, Transformers Are SSMs: Generalized Models and Efficient Algorithms Through Structured State Space Duality (Mamba-2, 2024).

Recent development & discussion

Lahoti et al., Mamba-3: Improved Sequence Modeling Using State Space Principles (2026).

Presentation focus

Unroll an SSM into a structured matrix and explain the restricted attention–SSM duality used by Mamba-2. Examine a state-tracking experiment from Mamba-3 and distinguish algorithmic complexity from measured hardware efficiency.

Topic 4Two academic hours

In-context learning as an algorithm

Sequence models & reasoning

How can a forward pass perform learning without changing the network's parameters?

Background & foundations

von Oswald et al., Transformers Learn In-Context by Gradient Descent (2023), selected sections on the linear-attention construction. Review least squares, ridge regression and Bayesian prediction for linear models.

Main paper

Akyürek et al., What Learning Algorithm Is In-Context Learning? Investigations with Linear Models (2023).

Recent development & discussion

Wurgaft et al., In-Context Learning Strategies Emerge Rationally (2025).

Presentation focus

Work through one regression-learning construction and compare gradient-descent, ridge and Bayesian predictors on a controlled task distribution. Separate existence constructions and observed behavior in trained synthetic-task models from claims about general language models.

Topic 5Two academic hours

Test-time training (TTT)

Sequence models & reasoning

What should a model learn at inference, and which objective makes those updates useful?

Scope for one session

Two academic hours: develop a recurrent TTT update, then examine end-to-end long-context training. Original distribution-shift TTT supplies the motivation and history; IT³ is supplementary reading.

Background & foundations

Sun, Wang, Liu, Miller, Efros & Hardt, Test-Time Training with Self-Supervision for Generalization under Distribution Shifts (ICML 2020), selected introduction and auxiliary-task formulation. Ba et al., Using Fast Weights to Attend to the Recent Past (2016), fast versus slow weights and associative memory.

Main paper

Sun et al., Learning to (Learn at Test Time): RNNs with Expressive Hidden States (2024). A model is the recurrent hidden state, and self-supervised gradient updates are the state transition.

Recent development & discussion

Tandon et al., End-to-End Test-Time Training for Long Context (2025). Next-token updates compress context into weights, with a meta-learned initialization. Supplementary adaptation example: Durasov, Shocher, Oner, Chechik, Efros & Fua, IT³: Idempotent Test-Time Training (ICML 2025); implementation.

Presentation focus

Start with the original 2020 adaptation problem, then work through a TTT-Linear update and its recurrent computation. Examine how end-to-end next-token training compresses context into weights, and explain the training and inference costs. IT³ provides a supplementary consistency-based adaptation example with its own training setup.

Topic 6Two academic hours

Recurrent depth and latent reasoning

Sequence models & reasoning

Can a model gain reasoning ability by repeatedly applying the same layers?

Background & foundations

Dehghani et al., Universal Transformers (ICLR 2019; 2018 preprint). Shared layers, recurrence over depth and adaptive computation.

Main paper

Geiping et al., Scaling up Test-Time Compute with Latent Reasoning: A Recurrent Depth Approach (NeurIPS 2025).

Recent development & discussion

Jolicoeur-Martineau, Less is More: Recursive Reasoning with Tiny Networks (2025). A short comparison with recursive refinement on puzzles.

Presentation focus

Trace one recurrent block, explain how it is trained across iteration counts, and assess performance against inference compute. Use tiny recursive models as a comparison. Distinguish iteration count, parameter count and demonstrated reasoning performance.

Topic 8Two academic hours

Online continual learning: stability, plasticity and replay

Representations, world models & continual learning

How can a network keep adapting to a changing data stream while retaining useful knowledge?

Scope for one session

Two academic hours: derive OCAR’s replay-constrained update, inspect one streaming experiment, and use CLA to connect prediction preservation with self-supervised representation alignment. A one-pass incoming stream can allow replay and bounded repeated updates on the current minibatch; it does not imply exactly one gradient step per observation.

Background & foundations

Chaudhry et al., On Tiny Episodic Memories in Continual Learning (2019): experience replay with limited memory. Kirkpatrick et al., Overcoming Catastrophic Forgetting in Neural Networks (PNAS 2017): elastic weight consolidation and Fisher-weighted protection. Optional mathematical background: Martens & Grosse, Optimizing Neural Networks with Kronecker-factored Approximate Curvature (ICML 2015), selected sections introducing the Fisher and one K-FAC block.

Main paper

Urettini & Carta, Online Curvature-Aware Replay: Leveraging 2nd Order Information for Online Continual Learning (ICML 2025). Focus on Sections 3–5: formulate the streaming update, constrain changes to predictions on replay data, and derive the curvature-aware step.

Recent development & discussion

Cignoni et al., CLA: Latent Alignment for Online Continual Self-Supervised Learning (CoLLAs 2026): compare representation alignment with labeled prediction constraints. Conceptual companion: Dohare et al., Loss of Plasticity in Deep Continual Learning (Nature 2024), which distinguishes forgetting old knowledge from losing the ability to learn new tasks; its broader continual-learning experiments are not all single-pass online protocols.

Presentation focus

Derive the local KL/Fisher constraint and the preconditioned update on a small example. Explain damping, memory representativeness and the difference between forgetting and loss of plasticity. Evaluate accuracy during the stream as well as at its end. Check what information each experiment provides: OCAR hides task boundaries, but its class-incremental replay-Fisher weighting uses the number of new classes per experience.

Topic 9Two academic hours

Platonic representations and unpaired alignment

Representations, world models & continual learning

Does shared representation geometry let us align independently trained models without paired alignment examples?

Scope for one session

Two academic hours: representation similarity, the Platonic hypothesis and one concrete unpaired alignment algorithm. Develop the Procrustes step on a small example; explain the full matching procedure without deriving every optimization subproblem.

Background & foundations

Kornblith et al., Similarity of Neural Network Representations Revisited (ICML 2019), centered kernel alignment. Huh et al., Position: The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (ICML 2024), the hypothesis and supporting experiments. Review an orthogonal Procrustes alignment using the SVD.

Main paper

Schnaus, Dagès, Cremers, Wang & Isola, Shared Geometry As A Rosetta Stone: Cross-Modal Alignment Without Paired Data (October 2026 preprint); the authors’ interactive project illustrates coarse cross-modal alignment without pairs at the alignment stage.

Recent development & discussion

Gröger, Wen & Brbic, Revisiting the Platonic Representation Hypothesis: An Aristotelian View (ICML 2026). Use the critique to examine which definitions and comparisons support claims about shared representations.

Presentation focus

Treat Rosetta as a practical test of a consequence of the Platonic hypothesis. Explain its coarse geometric initialization and alternating assignment / Procrustes updates, then examine the broad semantic structure it recovers and the finer details it misses. Paired samples are still used for evaluation and the CKA analysis of when alignment succeeds. Distinguish useful geometric correspondence from universal convergence or canonical features.

Topic 10Two academic hours

Hypernetworks and learning in weight space

Weight space & interpretability

How can a neural network generate, read or edit another network's weights while respecting their symmetries?

Scope for one session

Two academic hours: a hidden-neuron permutation example, one equivariant weight-processing layer, a selected expressivity result and a brief adapter-generation application. Basic symmetry is integrated background; general canonicalization topology is further reading.

Background & foundations

Ha, Dai & Le, HyperNetworks (ICLR 2017), a network generating another network's parameters. Navon, Shamsian, Achituve, Fetaya, Chechik & Maron, Equivariant Architectures for Learning in Deep Weight Spaces (ICML 2023), selected permutation action and DWS-layer construction; the authors' tutorial is an accessible introduction. Hu et al., LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models (2021/22), selected low-rank update formulation. Symmetry primer: Cohen & Welling, Group Equivariant Convolutional Networks (ICML 2016), group actions and equivariance definitions only. Apply these definitions to hidden-neuron permutations rather than developing a separate rotation-equivariant architecture.

Main paper

Dayan, Eitan & Maron, On the Expressive Power of Permutation-Equivariant Weight-Space Networks (ICML 2026). Focus on the approximation framework and one universality result for fixed-architecture MLP weight spaces: distinguish properties of parameterizations from properties of the functions those parameters realize.

Recent development & discussion

Liu, Wang, Mao, Gelberg, Maron & Zhang, SHINE: A Scalable In-Context Hypernetwork for Mapping Context to LoRA in a Single Pass (ICML 2026), a contemporary application that converts context into adapters. Supporting application reading: Charakorn et al., Doc-to-LoRA: Learning to Instantly Internalize Contexts (ICML 2026), amortized context distillation. Further mathematical reading: Dym, Lawrence & Siegel, Equivariant Frames and the Impossibility of Continuous Canonicalization (ICML 2024), for the continuity issue in the group-action settings studied there; its theorem is distinct from the MLP weight-space expressivity result.

Presentation focus

Derive the hidden-neuron permutation action for a small MLP and construct a simple equivariant weight-processing layer. Explain why arbitrary flattening obscures this structure. Define a canonical representative using the same neuron-permutation example, outline one universality result under a general-position assumption, and inspect a degenerate counterexample. Keep the broader continuity and topology proofs as further reading. End with a short SHINE example contrasting direct adapter generation with iterative parameter updates. SHINE does not instantiate the DWS construction; keep the MLP expressivity results separate from the empirical claims about LLM adaptation.

Topic 11Two academic hours

Model merging and pretrained weight neighborhoods

Weight space & interpretability

Which useful capabilities lie near a pretrained checkpoint, and how can we recover or combine them?

Scope for one session

Two academic hours: introduce task vectors and weight averaging, then center the seminar on Neural Thickets and the difference between combining weights and ensembling predictions.

Background & foundations

Ilharco et al., Editing Models with Task Arithmetic (2023), task vectors relative to a shared pretrained checkpoint. Wortsman et al., Model soups: averaging weights of multiple fine-tuned models improves accuracy without increasing inference time (ICML 2022), weight averaging versus prediction ensembles. Selected theory: Ortiz-Jimenez, Favero & Frossard, Task Arithmetic in the Tangent Space: Improved Editing of Pre-Trained Models (2023), the linearized model and weight disentanglement.

Main paper

Gan & Isola, Neural Thickets: Diverse Task Experts Are Dense Around Pretrained Weights (ICML 2026). RandOpt samples Gaussian weight perturbations, evaluates candidates on task data, selects experts and ensembles their predictions. See the authors’ illustrated project.

Recent development & discussion

Cendra et al., Can Bayesian Optimization Efficiently Find a Strong Single Expert in Neural Thickets? (2026), a follow-up in low-dimensional search settings. Empirical comparison: Hitit, Girrbach & Akata, A Systematic Study of In-the-Wild Model Merging for Large Language Models (TMLR 2026; 2025 preprint), heterogeneous checkpoints and conflicting objectives.

Presentation focus

Use function-space linearization to explain the intuition behind task-vector addition. Then develop expert density and diversity around a pretrained checkpoint, including the probability of finding a useful perturbation after repeated sampling. Compare weight averaging, expert selection and prediction ensembling, accounting for task-data evaluations and inference cost. These neighborhood results do not establish that random initialization or unrestricted gradient descent succeeds.

Topic 12Two academic hours

Mechanistic interpretability: from features to circuits

Weight space & interpretability

How do interpretable features support a circuit explanation, and how can we test that explanation causally?

Scope for one session

Two academic hours: introduce superposition and sparse features, then develop one circuit-tracing case study and causal intervention.

Background & foundations

Elhage et al., Toy Models of Superposition (2022), one small example of sparse features sharing dimensions. Selected background: Elhage et al., A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits (2021), the residual stream; Templeton et al., Scaling Monosemanticity: Extracting Interpretable Features from Claude 3 Sonnet (2024), the sparse-autoencoder objective and one feature illustration.

Main paper

Ameisen et al., Circuit Tracing: Revealing Computational Graphs in Language Models (2025).

Recent development & discussion

Kamath et al., Tracing Attention Computation Through Feature Interactions (2025). Limitation reading: Leask et al., Sparse Autoencoders Do Not Find Canonical Units of Analysis (2025), a caution about treating extracted features as canonical units.

Presentation focus

Use a toy example to explain superposition and introduce sparse feature extraction briefly. Center the presentation on how a replacement model produces an attribution graph, one circuit case study and a causal intervention. Distinguish sparse autoencoders from the tracing method’s replacement model. Assess omitted computations and approximation error; interpreting a feature and validating a circuit are different steps.

Topic 13Two academic hours

One-step generative models: Drifting and IMLE

Generative models

How can matching real and generated samples train a generator that produces a sample in one pass?

Scope for one session

One comparative seminar of two academic hours: derive Drifting as the central sample-matching mechanism, then compare IMLE’s nearest-neighbor assignments and ROMS-IMLE’s practical changes.

Background & foundations

Yujia Li, Swersky & Zemel, Generative Moment Matching Networks (ICML 2015), selected kernel/MMD material. Ke Li & Jitendra Malik, Implicit Maximum Likelihood Estimation (2018), the data-to-generated nearest-neighbor assignment and regression algorithm.

Main paper

Deng, He Li, Tianhong Li, Du & He, Generative Modeling via Drifting (2026). Estimate an attraction-repulsion field from real and generated samples and fit frozen drifted targets.

Recent development & discussion

Chirag Vashist & Ke Li, ROMS-IMLE: A Minimalist Approach to Competitive Single-Step Generative Modelling (2026). Compare nearest-neighbor assignment, robust losses and intermediate-resolution supervision with the drifting update. Optional theory: Esteban-Casadevall et al., Kernel-Gradient Drifting Models (2026), including Max Welling. The authors’ ROMS-IMLE method page provides additional explanation and experiments.

Presentation focus

Introduce sample-based distribution matching, derive the drifting field and frozen-target regression, and study one toy experiment. Compare IMLE’s data-to-generated matching and ROMS-IMLE’s practical changes, including the direction of nearest-neighbor assignment, robust losses and intermediate-resolution supervision. Keep zero-drift and maximum-likelihood claims tied to their assumptions.

Generative models

How can denoising generate discrete text, and what changes when generation need not proceed left to right?

Background & foundations

Topic 15Two academic hours

Oscillatory neurons and synchronization

Graphs & neural architectures

Can neural representations bind information through synchronization?

Scope for one session

Exploratory candidate connecting neural computation with dynamical systems, including work by Max Welling.

Background & foundations

Strogatz, From Kuramoto to Crawford: exploring the onset of synchronization in populations of coupled oscillators (2000). Sections 2 and 3.1–3.3 on coupled phases and synchronization (author-hosted PDF).

Main paper

Miyato, Löwe, Geiger & Welling, Artificial Kuramoto Oscillatory Neurons (ICLR 2025; 2024 preprint).

Recent development & discussion

Iqbal et al., Spontaneous symmetry breaking and Goldstone modes for deep information propagation (2026 preprint). A related perspective on symmetry and persistent information propagation, rather than a direct analysis of AKOrN.

Presentation focus

Derive the two-oscillator intuition, trace the generalized neural update, and study one object-discovery or reasoning experiment. Use the Goldstone paper for a brief connection to depth and memory; its physics derivations are optional.

Topic 16Two academic hours

GNNs: expressivity, information bottlenecks and graph transformers

Graphs & neural architectures

What limits message passing, and how do connectivity and structural information change what a graph network can learn?

Scope for one session

Two academic hours: two concrete failure examples, one structural-encoding mechanism and one graph-transformer experiment. Expander connectivity supplies a short comparison; full higher-order expressivity and spectral theory are further reading.

Background & foundations

Sanchez-Lengeling et al., A Gentle Introduction to Graph Neural Networks (Distill 2021), a visual primer. Xu, Hu, Leskovec & Jegelka, How Powerful are Graph Neural Networks? (ICLR 2019), multiset aggregation, GIN and the 1-Weisfeiler-Leman expressivity ceiling. Alon & Yahav, On the Bottleneck of Graph Neural Networks and its Practical Implications (ICLR 2021), one over-squashing example.

Main paper

Stoll, Müller & Morris, Generalizable Insights for Graph Transformers in Theory and Practice (NeurIPS 2025), selected theory sections and one structural-encoding ablation.

Recent development & discussion

Wilson, Bechler-Speicher & Veličković, Cayley Graph Propagation (2025), an intuitive expander-connectivity example and one propagation-cost comparison; the group-theory construction and spectral proofs are further reading. Ma et al., Plain Transformers Can be Powerful Graph Learners (TMLR 2026; 2025 preprint), selected structural-adaptation discussion. Further reading: Liang et al., Mitigating Over-Squashing in Graph Neural Networks by Spectrum-Preserving Sparsification (ICML 2025).

Presentation focus

Introduce message passing through a small graph. Work through one pair of graphs that 1-WL-style aggregation cannot distinguish and one narrow-cut graph that compresses distant information. Explain why expressivity, over-squashing and over-smoothing are different limitations. Center the main paper on how structural encodings affect graph transformers and one ablation. Use Cayley propagation briefly to compare changing connectivity with changing the model; avoid a second full presentation or claims that attention automatically solves either limitation.
